I'm a sucker for level editors. Excitebike was the first game I played that had one, and building my own tracks in it helped spark my curiosity about making games. In 2016 I had an experiment where you edited a course's line live in VR, and it never shipped. The anniversary edition has a course editor. It's still early days, but it gives anyone who wants one a way to add to the game.

What you can make

You can lay a new route over any of the four 2016 islands, or open one of the seven 2016 HoverJet missions and change it. You can make a new island too, from a seed and a few sliders or from a height map you bring yourself. Then you set the start pad, the finish and the time limit, move the sun, raise or lower the water, and place props from the game's own islands: the trees, rocks and cactus the 2016 missions use.

There are two ways to work. At a desk, you use a mouse over the island. In a headset, the island sits on a table in the hangar in front of your seat, and you grab the line and pull it around with your hands. That's where my 2016 VR experiment ended up. Both are the same editor underneath: one core holds the course and every change you can make to it, with undo and redo, and the desk and the headset are two ways of driving it.

Points, a spacing, a start and a finish

A 2016 mission is a scene file with every ring placed by hand. That's fine for thirteen missions made by one developer. It's no good for a player who wants to make a course and send it to a friend in a chat message. So a player's course is stored as the few things you'd need to rebuild it, not as a list of rings.

You place a handful of control points. The game draws a smooth line through them and hangs a ring on it every so many meters, and you choose how many. Add a start pad, a finish somewhere along the line and a time limit, and the course is done.

Try it: tap the stage a few times to lay a line out from the green pad. Watch the rings appear and the link grow by a few characters a point. Drag a point to make a hairpin and the editor warns you about the turn. Change ring spacing and every ring moves while the points stay where they are. delete last point takes the newest one off, and start again clears the lot.

The real editor goes a step further than this toy. It loads the island and judges every ring against the ground: a ring sunk into a hillside, or a line that dives under one, gets flagged before you share it. This page loads no terrain, so those ground warnings can't fire here.

Flying it

Press fly and you're in the course, flying it in the real game. When you finish or back out, you're in the editor where you left it, with the same point selected and the camera where it was. You can also start from any point along the line, to try one tricky turn without flying the whole thing. A clean lap from the start pad that takes every ring is kept as the author lap: proof that the course can be flown.

New islands from a seed

An island from a seed is made by a function, the same way every time. The course keeps the seed and four sliders, plus the version of the generator that made it, and wherever the course is opened, the game builds the island again from those numbers. The island flying around at the top of the prologue is one of these.

Try it: press new seed a few times. Drag rugged from smooth hills to sharp ridges, and how much sea from a big island to a small one in open water. Then try the sizes. Everything under what the course stores is all a link needs to make that island again.

How the island generator works

When I made the 2016 islands, I followed generic terrain tutorials and tweaked them until they looked right, probably with a little extra height for more verticality. The generator works the same way, in a few layers, each one built from noise.

Noise from a seed. Picture a grid of random numbers, one at every corner, with smooth blends between them. That's one layer of noise. Stack a few more on top, each one twice as fine and half as strong, and you get the lumpy-at-every-scale look of real ground. The seed decides which random numbers go on the grid. They're made with whole-number math and plain arithmetic only, so every computer and every browser builds the same island from the same seed, down to the last bit.

Bending. Before anything else, every point gets pushed sideways by another layer of noise, and then pushed again. That's what makes the coast and the ridges wander and braid instead of lining up with the grid.

The coast. The island is a circle in the middle of the square, with its edge pushed in and out by noise about every 1.4 km to make bays and headlands. Past the coast, the sea floor shelves off and then drops to the bottom.

The land. Three layers add up inside the coast: a dome that rises toward the middle, rolling hills about a kilometer across, and ridges. Ridges come from the same noise folded over where it crosses zero, which turns smooth bumps into sharp crests.

Low land, tall peaks. To make new islands look like they came from 2016, we measured the 2016 height maps for Simonet and Kauai. Their land sits low, with peaks that stand up tall. The generator gets that by squaring the height, which presses the lowlands toward the sea and leaves the peaks standing.

Crags and beaches. A last layer of fine ridges, 120 meters across and smaller, breaks up the high ground into crags and leaves the shore smooth. Then the lowest bit of land above the water is flattened toward it, which is the sandy ring you see around every island in the demo.

Sharing a course

      

        A course on a 2016 island or a seeded one, with up to 128 placed props, fits in a link.
        Everything else saves as a file. There's no server holding anybody's courses: the link or
        the file is the course.
      

      

        Every course from a player, whether it was made in the editor, opened from a link or read
        from a file, becomes a playable level through one function, and that function won't run on
        a course that doesn't validate. There's no second path for "trusted" courses.
